Cell Cycle and Mitosis Homework Help: Phases and Checkpoints

Follow the sequence from interphase through mitosis and cytokinesis.

Cell cycle homework often tests sequence and vocabulary: when DNA is copied, what happens during mitosis, and how checkpoints relate to progression. The key is to distinguish the broader cell cycle from mitosis itself and to use the phase names expected by your course.

Interphase prepares the cell

Interphase includes G1, S, and G2. A cell grows and carries out its functions in G1; DNA is replicated during S phase; and the cell continues preparation in G2. Interphase is part of the cell cycle, but it is not one of the mitotic phases.

Track the main mitotic events

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense and the mitotic spindle begins to form.
  • Prometaphase: in courses that name it separately, the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle microtubules attach to chromosomes.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes align at the cell’s equatorial region.
  • Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and move toward opposite poles.
  • Telophase: nuclei re-form around the separated chromosome sets.

Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm and is often taught alongside or after mitosis. Some course diagrams combine or label events differently, so follow the terminology in your lecture and assignment.

Worked sequence question

What follows DNA replication?

If a cell has completed DNA replication in S phase, it proceeds through G2 before entering mitosis, assuming it passes the relevant controls. During mitosis, sister chromatids separate at anaphase. This is not the same event as homologous chromosome separation in meiosis I.

Where checkpoints fit

Cell cycle control includes checks around the G1/S transition, before mitosis at G2/M, and during chromosome attachment and alignment in mitosis. Textbooks may use terms such as spindle checkpoint or metaphase checkpoint for the mitotic control. Explain what is being checked—conditions for DNA replication, completion of replication and readiness to divide, or proper chromosome attachment—rather than memorizing checkpoint names alone.

How to solve diagram and phase problems

  1. Look for whether chromosomes are replicated and condensed.
  2. Check whether they are aligned, separating, or enclosed by new nuclei.
  3. Identify whether the diagram shows nuclear division or cytokinesis.
  4. Label sister chromatids and poles before drawing arrows or describing movement.
