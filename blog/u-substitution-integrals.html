U-Substitution in Integration: When to Use It and Worked Steps

Part of the College Calculus Survival Guide

Use u-substitution when an integral contains a composite function and the remaining factor matches, or is a constant multiple of, its derivative. The key is to check that the substitution accounts for the whole integrand, including the differential. This guide walks through that check, a case where a constant must be factored out, and a definite integral where the bounds change.

How to know when to use u-substitution

Look for a function inside another function, multiplied by its derivative or a constant multiple. For example, in 2x cos(x²), the inner function is x² and its derivative, 2x, is also present.

A useful test is to temporarily name the inner expression u and differentiate it. If the remaining factor can become du after a simple constant adjustment, substitution is promising. If a substantial x-expression remains that cannot be rewritten in terms of u, a different method may be needed.

U-substitution integration steps

  1. Choose u — usually the inside function (what is inside parentheses, under a root, or in an exponent).
  2. Find du by differentiating u with respect to x, then write du = ... dx.
  3. Rewrite the entire integral in terms of u, replacing every x-term, including dx.
  4. Integrate with respect to u using standard rules.
  5. Substitute back to express the answer in terms of x (unless you already changed the bounds — see below).

Worked example

Evaluate ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx

Let u = x², so du = 2x dx — which conveniently matches the 2x already in the integral.

The integral becomes ∫ cos(u) du = sin(u) + C

Substituting back: sin(x²) + C

When the derivative differs by a constant

The derivative does not have to appear exactly as written; a constant multiple can be factored out. For example, evaluate ∫ x(x² + 1)³ dx.

  1. Let u = x² + 1, so du = 2x dx and x dx = du/2.
  2. Rewrite the integral as (1/2)∫ u³ du.
  3. Integrate: (1/2)(u&sup4;/4) + C = u&sup4;/8 + C.
  4. Substitute back: (x² + 1)&sup4;/8 + C.

Differentiate the result to check it: the chain rule gives (1/8) · 4(x² + 1)³ · 2x = x(x² + 1)³, the original integrand.

Definite integrals: change the bounds too

For a definite integral, either convert both bounds to u-values and finish in u, or return to x before using the original bounds. Do not mix the two approaches.

Worked definite integral

Evaluate ∫ from 0 to 1 of 2x cos(x²) dx. Set u = x², so du = 2x dx. Convert the bounds: when x = 0, u = 0; when x = 1, u = 1.

The integral becomes ∫ from 0 to 1 of cos(u) du = sin(1) - sin(0) = sin(1) ≈ 0.8415.

Because the bounds were changed to u-values, do not substitute x back into this final evaluation.

Common u-substitution mistakes

Forgetting to substitute dx. Every part of the original integral needs to be rewritten in terms of u, including dx — not just the obvious “inside function” piece.

Not changing the bounds on a definite integral. If you’re evaluating from a to b, either convert the bounds to u-values right when you substitute, or make sure to substitute back to x before plugging in the original bounds. Mixing the two approaches is a very common error.
