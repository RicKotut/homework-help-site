How to Write a Literature Review

A step-by-step guide to finding, organizing, and synthesizing research for a college paper, thesis, or dissertation.

A literature review is more than a stack of summaries. It explains what credible sources collectively say about a focused question, where researchers agree or differ, and what the evidence still leaves unresolved. The same core process works for a short research-paper assignment and a longer thesis or dissertation chapter; the scope and depth change to match your instructions.

If you are looking for literature review help, begin by turning the prompt into a question you can actually research. Then read sources with a comparison in mind. Purdue OWL’s literature review guide also emphasizes synthesis, evaluation, and organization rather than one-source-at-a-time summaries.

1. Define the question and boundaries

Underline the task words in your prompt: review, compare, evaluate, or identify a gap. Write a one-sentence question that names the topic, population or setting, and time period when those details matter. For example, “What does recent research say about the effect of retrieval practice on exam performance among college students?” is easier to search than “How do people learn?”

Set boundaries before collecting sources. Your instructor may specify a date range, number of scholarly sources, discipline, or citation style. A literature review for a research paper is usually narrower than a standalone review or dissertation chapter. Follow the assignment rubric and ask your instructor if the scope is unclear.

2. Search for sources you can evaluate

Start with your college library database and combine the main ideas in your question with synonyms. Try several combinations, such as “retrieval practice,” “active recall,” “college students,” and “exam performance.” Use filters for scholarly or peer-reviewed material when appropriate, then check the abstract and reference list to find relevant studies.

Keep a source log as you search. Record the citation, research question, method, sample or material studied, main finding, limitation, and how the source might fit your review. This saves time when you build an outline and helps you distinguish sources that merely mention a topic from sources that answer your question.

3. Compare sources in a literature review matrix

A simple matrix makes patterns visible. Give each source a row and use columns for its question, method, participants or data, findings, limitations, and connection to other studies. Do not copy whole abstracts into the table; write brief notes in your own words and add citation details immediately.

A synthesis pattern

“Several studies found that short retrieval quizzes were associated with stronger delayed recall. However, the studies used different course subjects and quiz schedules, so the evidence does not establish one ideal schedule for every class.”

This connects findings and notes a limitation. A paragraph that reports one article after another without comparison is still a list of summaries.

4. Organize the review around ideas

Choose an order that helps the reader understand the evidence. A thematic structure groups studies by recurring concepts or findings. A methodological structure compares research designs. A chronological structure can show how thinking changed over time, but it should still explain why those changes matter. Many reviews combine approaches.

Build each section around a point, not an author. Start with a topic sentence, compare two or more sources, explain meaningful differences, and connect the paragraph back to your review question. Use transitions such as “in contrast,” “similarly,” and “a limitation across these studies” only when the relationship is accurate.

5. Draft, cite, and revise

Introduce the scope and explain how the review is organized. In the body, synthesize evidence and evaluate its strengths and limitations. In the conclusion, summarize what the literature suggests and how that answer relates to your research question. Avoid claiming that a small set of studies proves more than it does.

Cite ideas, findings, and wording that came from sources, including paraphrases. For help with attribution, see how to cite sources and avoid plagiarism. On revision, check that every paragraph supports the question, transitions show real relationships, and each in-text citation matches the required style.

Common literature review mistakes

Summarizing sources one by one. Group them by shared findings, methods, or disagreement, then explain the pattern.

Using a question that is too broad. Narrow the subject, population, setting, or time period until the assignment can be answered with the available sources.

Leaving citations until the end. Track citation information while reading so borrowed ideas do not become hard to trace.
