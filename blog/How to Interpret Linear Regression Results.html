How to Interpret Linear Regression Results for College Statistics

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • The Slope (b₁ / Coefficient): Tells you how much Y (dependent variable) changes for every 1-unit increase in X (independent variable).
  • R² (R-Squared): The proportion of variance in Y explained by your regression model (0 to 100%).
  • p-value (Significance): Tests if the relationship is statistically significant. If p < 0.05, reject the null hypothesis and conclude that X is a significant predictor of Y.

Linear regression is one of the most frequently tested concepts in introductory and intermediate college statistics courses. Whether your professor requires output from SPSS, R, Excel, or Stata, the core challenge is almost always the same: translating a grid of raw numbers into clear, plain-English statements.

In this guide, we break down the 4 key numbers in any linear regression table, show you how to construct the regression line equation, and walk through a complete real-world example step-by-step—the exact same way our tutors explain it in 1-on-1 sessions.

The Simple Linear Regression Equation

Simple linear regression models the relationship between one quantitative independent variable (X, the predictor) and one quantitative dependent variable (Y, the outcome):

Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X

  • Ŷ (Y-hat): The predicted value of the outcome variable.
  • b₀ (Intercept / Constant): The predicted value of Y when X = 0.
  • b₁ (Slope / Coefficient): The estimated change in Y for every 1-unit increase in X.

The 4 Numbers That Matter Most in Regression Output

When you run a regression model in statistical software like SPSS, Excel, or R, you will receive several tables. Focus on these four core metrics:

1. The Coefficient (b₁)

The regression coefficient for your predictor variable tells you the direction and strength of the linear relationship.

  • Interpretation template: "For every 1-unit increase in [X], [Y] is predicted to increase/decrease by [b₁] units, holding all other variables constant."

2. R² (Coefficient of Determination)

Located in the Model Summary table, R² measures model fit, ranging from 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%).

  • Interpretation template: "[R² × 100]% of the variation in [Y] is explained by the linear relationship with [X]."

3. The p-value (p or Sig.)

Tests the null hypothesis that the slope coefficient equals zero (H₀: β₁ = 0).

  • If p < 0.05: The predictor is statistically significant.
  • If p ≥ 0.05: Insufficient evidence to conclude a significant linear relationship exists.

4. The Intercept (b₀)

The constant term represents the baseline value of Y when X equals zero.

Step-by-Step Worked Example

A researcher wants to predict student exam scores (Y) based on weekly study hours (X).

Model Term Unstandardized Coefficient (b) Std. Error t-statistic p-value (Sig.)
(Constant / Intercept) 52.40 3.12 16.79 < .001
Study Hours (X) 3.85 0.45 8.55 < .001

Model Summary: R² = 0.642 (64.2%)

Step 1: Write the Regression Line Equation

Exam Score = 52.40 + 3.85 × (Study Hours)

Step 2: Interpret the Slope Coefficient

"For each additional hour a student studies per week, their exam score is predicted to increase by 3.85 points."

Step 3: Interpret R²

"Approximately 64.2% of the variance in exam scores can be explained by the number of hours spent studying per week."

Step 4: Evaluate Statistical Significance

"There is a statistically significant positive linear relationship between study hours and exam performance (p < .001)."

Common Mistakes That Lose Exam Points

  1. Confusing Correlation (r) with Regression (R²): Correlation measures association strength; regression predicts outcome values and variance explained.
  2. Misinterpreting Causation: Regression proves association, not direct causality.
  3. Extrapolation: Don't predict values far outside your dataset's range.
  4. Ignoring Assumptions: Ensure linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between simple and multiple linear regression?

Simple linear regression uses one independent variable (X), while multiple regression uses two or more predictors (X₁, X₂, ...).

What should I do if my p-value is greater than 0.05?

The predictor is not statistically significant in your model; you fail to reject the null hypothesis.
