What Does a P-Value Less Than 0.05 Actually Mean?

Part of the College Statistics Survival Guide

The 0.05 threshold gets used constantly in intro statistics, but the correct interpretation of a p-value is one of the most commonly misunderstood ideas in the entire course — including, frankly, by plenty of people well past their intro course.

The setup: null and alternative hypotheses

Every hypothesis test starts with a null hypothesis (usually "no difference" or "no effect") and an alternative hypothesis (there IS a difference or effect). The p-value is calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true.

What a p-value actually is

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as what you got, if the null hypothesis were actually true. That's it. It's a statement about how surprising your data would be under the assumption of "no effect" — not a statement about whether there's actually an effect.

Why 0.05 specifically

0.05 is a convention, not a law of nature. It means: if the null hypothesis is true, there's only a 5% chance of seeing data this extreme by random chance alone. When your p-value is below that threshold, the result is considered "statistically significant" — unlikely enough under the null hypothesis that you reject it in favor of the alternative.

Worked example

A t-test comparing two teaching methods returns p = 0.03.

Correct interpretation: if there were truly no difference between the methods, there'd only be a 3% chance of seeing a difference this large in your sample by chance alone. Since 0.03 < 0.05, you'd reject the null hypothesis and conclude there's a statistically significant difference.

Common mistakes

"The p-value is the probability the null hypothesis is true." This is the single most common misinterpretation, and it's wrong. The p-value assumes the null hypothesis is true from the start — it can't also be telling you the probability of that assumption.

Treating "statistically significant" as "important." A large enough sample size can make even a tiny, practically meaningless difference statistically significant. Always look at the effect size alongside the p-value, not the p-value alone.
