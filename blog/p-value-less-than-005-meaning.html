What Does a P-Value Less Than 0.05 Actually Mean?

Part of the College Statistics Survival Guide

"p < 0.05" is one of the most repeated phrases in any intro statistics course, and also one of the most commonly misunderstood. Getting p-value interpretation right matters more than memorizing the cutoff — here's p-value explained the way we'd walk through it in a statistics homework help session, without the textbook jargon.

What a p-value actually measures

A p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis and the test’s other assumptions are true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed. “As extreme” is defined by the statistical test and whether it is one-sided or two-sided. A p-value describes how the data compare with that model; it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Why 0.05 is the cutoff (and why it's somewhat arbitrary)

If a test uses a significance level α = 0.05, a p-value below 0.05 meets that preselected decision threshold. The threshold is a convention chosen before evaluating results, not a universal boundary between “true” and “false.” Other studies may use a different α based on their field and the consequences of errors.

What a small p-value does NOT mean

  • It's not the probability the null hypothesis is true. This is the single most common misinterpretation in hypothesis testing — a p-value of 0.03 does not mean there's a 3% chance the null hypothesis is correct.
  • It doesn't measure how big or important the effect is. A tiny, practically meaningless effect can still produce a very small p-value if the sample size is large enough.
  • p = 0.049 is not meaningfully different from p = 0.051. Treating 0.05 as a hard line where one side is "real" and the other isn't misses the point of what the number represents.

Worked example

A study compares exam scores between students who used a new study app and those who didn't, and finds a p-value of 0.03 for the difference in average scores.

Interpretation: if the app truly had no effect and the test assumptions hold, the probability of results at least this extreme under the null model is 0.03. If the study selected α = 0.05 in advance, it would reject the null hypothesis under that rule.

What it does not tell you: the probability that the app works, the size of the score difference, or whether any difference matters in practice. To assess those, report the estimated difference and an appropriate confidence interval or effect size alongside the test result.

A practical way to interpret a test result

  1. State the hypotheses. Identify the null claim and the alternative being tested, in the context of the assignment or research question.
  2. Check the test and assumptions. Confirm that the chosen test, its direction (one- or two-sided), and its assumptions fit the data and question.
  3. Compare p with the preselected α. If p is below α, describe the result as statistically significant under that decision rule; otherwise, say the evidence was not sufficient to reject the null.
  4. Describe the size and uncertainty. Include the estimated effect and a confidence interval where appropriate. Statistical significance alone does not show practical importance.

For example, report the test statistic, degrees of freedom when relevant, exact p-value (unless it is smaller than the software can display), effect estimate, and interval according to your course’s required style. Avoid writing “p = 0.000”; software rounding does not mean the probability is literally zero.

Why p-values near 0.05 need careful wording

A result of p = 0.049 and one of p = 0.051 are nearly the same amount of evidence under their respective null models, even though they fall on different sides of α = 0.05. Report the actual value and the estimate; avoid treating the cutoff as proof that one effect is real and the other is absent.

Common mistakes

Reporting significance without reporting effect size. A statistically significant result with a tiny practical effect can be misleading if effect size isn't mentioned alongside it.

Treating "not significant" as "no effect." A p-value above 0.05 means you don't have strong enough evidence to rule out the null hypothesis — not that you've proven there's no effect at all.
