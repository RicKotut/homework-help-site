Statistics Methods & Software

Probability Basics: Sample Space, Mutually Exclusive Events and More

Part of the Statistics Software and Methods Guide

Probability theory is the foundation under every test in a statistics course, and the rules are short. Most mistakes come from using the right formula in the wrong situation, so this guide pairs each rule with the question that tells you when it applies.

The vocabulary

  • Experiment: something with an uncertain result, such as rolling a die.
  • Outcome: one possible result, such as rolling a 4.
  • Sample space: the set of all possible outcomes. For one die, {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
  • Event: a set of outcomes you care about, such as “rolling an even number.”

The basic rules

  • A probability is always between 0 and 1.
  • When outcomes are equally likely: P(event) = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.
  • The complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Relative frequency is what actually happened: the number of times an event occurred ÷ the number of trials. With many trials it approaches the theoretical probability.

Combining events

  • Mutually exclusive events can’t happen together: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
  • If they can overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
  • Independent events don’t affect each other: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • Conditional probability: P(A given B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B).

Worked examples with one die

Basic: P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5.

Mutually exclusive: P(roll a 1 or a 2) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 ≈ 0.33.

Overlapping: P(even or greater than 4). Even = {2, 4, 6}. Greater than 4 = {5, 6}. They share {6}. So 3/6 + 2/6 − 1/6 = 4/6 ≈ 0.67.

Independent: P(two heads in two coin tosses) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.

Conditional: P(even given greater than 3). Greater than 3 = {4, 5, 6}, and the even outcomes among them are {4, 6}. So (2/6) ÷ (3/6) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67.

Relative frequency: if 100 tosses give 46 heads, the relative frequency is 0.46, close to the theoretical 0.5.

Common mistakes

Adding probabilities of overlapping events. If the events can happen together, subtract the overlap, or you count it twice.

Mixing up mutually exclusive and independent. They are different ideas. Two events that can’t both happen (and each have a nonzero probability) are actually not independent, because knowing one occurred tells you the other didn’t.

Treating a small sample’s relative frequency as the true probability. Ten tosses can easily give 7 heads. It tells you little about the coin.
