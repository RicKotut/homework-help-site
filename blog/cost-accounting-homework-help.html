Cost Accounting Homework Help: Job and Process Costing

A step-by-step way to apply overhead and check the total cost of a job.

Cost accounting homework often asks you to trace materials, labor, and overhead through a production process. The arithmetic is manageable once you identify the costing system and the correct allocation base. This guide explains job-order and process costing, then works through a predetermined overhead example. For assignment-specific guidance, Study Hall Tutors offers cost accounting homework help that focuses on the steps and reasoning.

Job-order costing or process costing?

Use job-order costing when products or services are distinct and costs are accumulated by job, batch, or client. A job cost sheet records direct materials, direct labor, and manufacturing overhead applied to that job.

Use process costing when many similar units move through continuous production departments. Costs are accumulated by department and period, then averaged across equivalent units. If a problem gives beginning or ending work in process, check whether it specifies weighted-average or FIFO; the equivalent-unit steps differ.

Calculate a predetermined overhead rate

Because many overhead costs are not known until the end of a period, courses commonly use a predetermined rate based on estimates:

Core formula

Predetermined overhead rate = estimated manufacturing overhead ÷ estimated amount of the allocation base

Overhead applied to a job = predetermined rate × actual amount of the base used by that job

The allocation base might be machine hours, direct labor hours, or another measure specified by the problem. Match the units in the numerator, denominator, and job data.

Worked example: find the total cost of one job

Job-order costing

A company estimates manufacturing overhead of $180,000 and 12,000 machine hours for the year. Job 27 uses 20 machine hours, $950 of direct materials, and $600 of direct labor.

  1. Rate = $180,000 ÷ 12,000 machine hours = $15 per machine hour.
  2. Overhead applied to Job 27 = 20 machine hours × $15 = $300.
  3. Total job cost = $950 direct materials + $600 direct labor + $300 applied overhead = $1,850.

Before moving on, check that the 20 hours are the job’s actual machine hours and that all three cost categories belong to manufacturing.

Common cost accounting homework errors

  • Using actual overhead to cost an unfinished job. If the question asks for applied overhead during the period, use the predetermined rate unless it specifies otherwise.
  • Choosing the wrong base. A labor-hour rate cannot be multiplied by machine hours. Read the rate’s units.
  • Leaving out one product cost. Total manufacturing cost includes direct materials, direct labor, and applied manufacturing overhead.
  • Mixing product and period costs. Selling and administrative expenses are generally period costs, not manufacturing costs assigned to inventory.
  • Rounding too early. Keep the rate precise until the final result unless the assignment specifies a rounding rule.

How to show your work clearly

Label the system, write the rate formula with units, show the overhead applied, and then add the three manufacturing cost components. For process costing, show equivalent units and the cost per equivalent unit before assigning costs to completed units and ending work in process. Follow the method your course requires.
