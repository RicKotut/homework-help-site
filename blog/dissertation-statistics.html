Dissertation Statistics: Choosing and Interpreting Methods

A planning framework for connecting your research question, data, analysis, and written results.

Dissertation statistics can feel like a separate technical hurdle, but the analysis should follow from the research question and study design. Before opening SPSS, R, Excel, or another package, be clear about what each variable represents, what comparison or relationship you want to examine, and what your program expects you to report.

This guide is for students learning to plan and interpret their own work. Dissertation statistics help should clarify decisions and explain results; your advisor, committee, and institutional requirements remain authoritative for your project.

1. Translate each research question into an analysis task

List each research question separately and identify its outcome. Is it asking you to describe a sample, compare groups, estimate an association, predict an outcome, or explore participants’ accounts? Write the question in plain language before naming a statistical test.

For example, “Do average satisfaction scores differ between two independent groups?” suggests a comparison question. “Is weekly study time associated with exam score?” asks about a relationship. The final method depends on more than that wording, but the distinction helps narrow the options.

2. Make a variable map

For each question, record the outcome variable, predictor or grouping variable, how each is measured, and the number of groups or time points. Note whether values are continuous, categorical, counts, repeated measurements, or another form. Also record where the data came from and any inclusion or exclusion rules.

This step catches common mismatches early. A method designed for independent observations may not fit repeated measurements from the same participants. A score coded into categories is not analyzed in exactly the same way as a continuous measure. If the dataset has missing values or small subgroups, document those facts rather than hiding them.

3. Choose a candidate method, then check its assumptions

Use your research question, design, variable types, and course or program guidance to identify a candidate analysis. A test-selection chart can help orient you; see how to choose a statistical test. It is a starting point, not a replacement for your methods course or advisor’s review.

Check the assumptions that apply to the chosen method using the procedure taught in your program. Depending on the analysis, these may involve independence, distribution shape, equal variance, linearity, or sample structure. Do not mechanically select a different test just because one diagnostic looks unusual. Explain the concern and ask your advisor or statistician how it affects the planned analysis.

4. Plan sample size and missing-data decisions with your advisor

Do not choose a sample size by copying a number from another dissertation. The appropriate plan depends on the research question, design, expected effect or precision, analysis, available population, and ethical or practical limits. A power analysis may be appropriate for some quantitative designs, but its inputs need justification.

Decide in advance how you will describe missing data and what rules will guide exclusions or data cleaning. Keep a record of those decisions so the methods section accurately describes what happened. If a decision changes after you inspect the data, disclose the reason and discuss it with your advisor.

5. Interpret output in context

Start with the analysis question and locate the relevant estimate, uncertainty measure, test statistic, and p-value if the method reports one. Then explain the direction and size of the result in words tied to the study variables. A p-value is not the probability that your hypothesis is true, and statistical significance does not automatically mean an effect is important in practice.

For regression, for example, distinguish the estimated association from a causal claim unless the design supports causal inference. Our guide to interpreting regression results walks through coefficients, p-values, and R-squared. For a confidence interval, explain the estimated range and its uncertainty without describing it as a range that contains a fixed percentage of individual observations.

6. Write results so another reader can follow

Use the reporting format required by your department or style guide. State which analysis addressed each question, report the key results accurately, and include tables only when they clarify the findings. Keep interpretation of meaning and implications in the section your program expects, often the discussion. USC’s research results guide recommends focused reporting and separating results from broader interpretation.

Questions to bring to an advisor or statistician

  • Does this analysis fit the research question and study design?
  • Which assumptions should I check, and how should I report them?
  • How should I justify the sample size and handle missing data?
  • Which effect-size or uncertainty measures does my field expect?
  • Does my program require a specific reporting style or software?
