How to Cite Sources and Avoid Plagiarism in College Writing

A practical guide to citations, paraphrasing, quotations, and responsible use of research.

Citations do more than fill a reference page. They show which ideas came from other writers, let readers find the sources, and help you make your own contribution clear. If you are looking for citation help, use this routine while you research instead of trying to reconstruct sources at the end.

Always follow the citation style and academic-integrity policy your instructor or department assigns. APA, MLA, Chicago, and other styles differ in their details. APA Style’s citation guidance explains how in-text citations identify sources, including paraphrases and direct quotations.

When should you cite a source?

Cite a source when you use its specific idea, finding, argument, wording, data, image, or other material. A paraphrase still needs attribution because changing the wording does not make the underlying idea yours. Common knowledge may not need a citation, but what counts as common knowledge can vary by subject and assignment. When you are unsure, check the course guide or ask your instructor.

How to paraphrase accurately

  1. Read the passage until you understand the point, then look away from the source.
  2. Explain the idea in your own sentence structure and wording, keeping its meaning accurate.
  3. Compare your version with the original to check that you have not copied distinctive phrasing.
  4. Add the required in-text citation immediately, then record the full source details.

A thesaurus-based word swap is not a reliable paraphrase. It can keep the source’s sentence structure and obscure how much wording was borrowed. APA’s guide to avoiding plagiarism recommends acknowledging words and ideas from other sources, including when paraphrased.

When and how to use a direct quotation

Quote when the exact wording matters, such as when analyzing a definition or a particularly precise statement. Use quotation marks or the block-quote format required by your style guide, cite the source, and include a page or location when the style requires it. Introduce the quotation and explain how it supports your point; a quotation should not do the work of your analysis.

Long strings of quotations can make an essay feel like a source collection rather than your argument. Prefer paraphrase for ordinary supporting points and quote selectively.

Keep a source log as you research

Save the author, title, publication date, journal or publisher, DOI or URL, and page numbers when relevant. Also note what you want to use from the source and where it may fit. Keep direct quotations clearly marked in your notes so you do not later mistake copied wording for your own draft.

Match in-text citations and references

Use the citation style assigned for the paper. In many author-date systems, the in-text citation points readers to a matching entry in the reference list. Other styles use notes or numbered citations. Check capitalization, punctuation, author order, dates, page or section locators, and DOI or URL formatting against an official style guide or your course materials.

For a literature review, citation tracking is especially useful because you compare many sources across several sections. See our step-by-step literature review guide for a source matrix and synthesis workflow. Biology students can also refer to the biology lab report guide when organizing references for a lab assignment.

What plagiarism checks can and cannot tell you

A similarity report flags matching text; it does not by itself determine whether plagiarism occurred. Common phrases, properly quoted passages, reference entries, and assignment templates may also match. Review flagged text, compare it with the source, and correct missing attribution or quotation formatting. Follow your institution’s policy and ask your instructor how to interpret a report.

Quick citation checklist

  • Have I cited every borrowed idea, fact, finding, and direct quotation that needs attribution?
  • Are paraphrases genuinely in my own words and sentence structure?
  • Are quotations clearly marked and accurately copied?
  • Does each in-text citation connect to the correct full reference or note?
  • Did I follow the exact style and academic-integrity rules for this assignment?
