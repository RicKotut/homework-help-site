Audit Assignment Help: Assertions, Risks and Procedures

A framework for showing how audit evidence addresses a specific risk.

Auditing assignments often ask you to identify a risk, connect it to a financial statement assertion, and propose a procedure that would gather relevant evidence. Listing generic audit steps is not enough: explain what the procedure tests and how the result would address the risk. This guide walks through that reasoning and common assertion distinctions. Identify the account, risk, and assertion

Start by naming the account or disclosure affected and the possible misstatement. Then identify the assertion most directly at risk. Common assertions include:

  • Existence or occurrence: recorded assets exist, or recorded transactions occurred.
  • Completeness: items that should be recorded have not been omitted.
  • Accuracy, valuation, and allocation: amounts and related estimates are recorded appropriately.
  • Rights and obligations: the entity owns the asset or is responsible for the liability.
  • Presentation and disclosure: items are classified and described in line with the reporting framework.

Use the assertion names and definitions from your course materials, since courses may group or label them differently for transactions, balances, and disclosures.

Worked example: accounts receivable existence

Link the risk to evidence

Risk: recorded receivables include sales that did not occur, overstating the balance.

Assertion: existence of accounts receivable at the reporting date.

Possible procedure: select recorded customer balances and send confirmations, then investigate differences. For a nonresponse, examine subsequent cash receipts and supporting sales and shipping documents as appropriate to the case.

Why it fits: the work starts from amounts in the ledger and seeks evidence that those recorded balances represent real amounts owed. The exact procedure should match the assignment facts, audit standards taught, and available evidence.

Completeness uses a different direction

To test completeness of recorded sales, start with evidence that a sale should have been recorded, such as sequential shipping documents, and trace selected items forward to the sales journal or ledger. This direction differs from starting at recorded sales and vouching back to source documents, which is more directly aimed at occurrence. In an assignment, state the starting population and the direction of testing.

Make an audit procedure specific

A strong procedure names the population or sample, the evidence to inspect, and the comparison or conclusion. For example, “inspect subsequent cash receipts for selected year-end customer balances and compare the amount and date to the receivable listing” is more specific than “check receivables.” Your instructor may also expect the procedure’s timing, sample method, or documentation.

Common auditing homework mistakes

  • Using “accuracy” when the risk is actually that transactions were omitted, which points toward completeness.
  • Proposing a procedure without explaining which assertion or misstatement it addresses.
  • Confusing inquiry with stronger corroborating evidence when the prompt asks for evidence of a balance.
  • Writing a procedure that is too broad to show what records, transactions, or dates will be examined.
  • Claiming that one sample or test proves the entire financial statement is free from error.

For case-based audit questions, use the facts in the scenario to prioritize risk. Avoid adding assumptions that the case does not give you.
