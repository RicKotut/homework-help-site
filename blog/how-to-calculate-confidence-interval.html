How to Calculate a Confidence Interval Step by Step

Part of the College Statistics Survival Guide

A confidence interval gives you a range of plausible values for a population parameter, instead of just a single estimate. The calculation itself is fairly mechanical once you know the pieces — the part that trips people up is picking the right critical value and interpreting the result correctly afterward.

The formula

Confidence interval = point estimate ± margin of error

For a sample mean, that's typically: x̄ ± (critical value) × (standard error)

The step-by-step method

  1. Find your sample mean (x̄) and sample standard deviation (s).
  2. Calculate the standard error: s / √n
  3. Find the critical value for your confidence level — use a z-value for large samples with known population standard deviation, or a t-value (from the t-distribution, using n−1 degrees of freedom) for smaller samples.
  4. Calculate the margin of error: critical value × standard error
  5. Construct the interval: sample mean ± margin of error

Worked example

A sample of 36 students has a mean study time of 8 hours/week, with a standard deviation of 3 hours. Find a 95% confidence interval for the true mean.

Standard error = 3 / √36 = 0.5

For a 95% confidence level with a large sample, the critical z-value is approximately 1.96.

Margin of error = 1.96 × 0.5 = 0.98

Confidence interval = 8 ± 0.98 = (7.02, 8.98)

We're 95% confident the true population mean falls between 7.02 and 8.98 hours per week.

Common mistakes

Using a z-value when you should use a t-value. For smaller samples (roughly under 30) or when the population standard deviation is unknown, the t-distribution is more appropriate and gives a wider, more accurate interval.

Misinterpreting what "95% confidence" means. It does NOT mean "there's a 95% chance the true mean is in this specific interval." The correct interpretation: if you repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population mean.
