College Statistics

How to Know Which Statistical Test to Use

Part of the College Statistics Survival Guide

Choosing the wrong statistical test means every number you calculate afterward is answering a question you weren't actually asked. The good news: the choice usually comes down to answering two or three simple questions about your data, not memorizing a huge table.

Start with these questions

  1. What type of data do you have? Categorical (groups/labels) or continuous (numbers on a scale)?
  2. How many groups are you comparing? One, two, or three or more?
  3. Are the groups independent or paired? (Different people vs. the same people measured twice.)
  4. Are you comparing groups, or looking at a relationship between variables?

A simple decision path

  • Comparing the means of 2 independent groups — use an independent-samples t-test.
  • Comparing the means of the same group before/after — use a paired t-test.
  • Comparing means across 3+ groups — use ANOVA, not multiple t-tests.
  • Comparing categorical data across groups (e.g. pass/fail rates) — use a chi-square test.
  • Looking at the relationship between two continuous variables — use correlation or regression.

Worked example

You want to know if average exam scores differ between 3 different teaching methods.

You have one continuous variable (exam score) and one categorical variable with 3 groups (teaching method). That's 3+ groups being compared on a continuous outcome — use one-way ANOVA, not three separate t-tests.

Common mistakes

Running multiple t-tests instead of one ANOVA. Comparing 3 groups with 3 separate t-tests inflates your chance of a false positive (Type I error) well above 5%. One ANOVA test is the statistically correct choice for 3+ groups.

Treating paired data as independent. If the same people were measured twice (before/after a treatment, for example), that's paired data — using an independent-samples test instead of a paired test ignores the connection between the measurements and can give a misleading result.
