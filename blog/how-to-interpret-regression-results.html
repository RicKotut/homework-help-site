College Statistics

How to Interpret Regression Results

Part of the College Statistics Survival Guide

Running a regression is usually the easy part — software does the calculation for you. The skill your assignment is actually testing is whether you can read the output and explain what it means, which is a different skill entirely from producing it.

The key pieces of regression output

  • Coefficient (slope): how much the outcome variable changes for each 1-unit increase in the predictor.
  • Intercept: the predicted value of the outcome when the predictor equals zero.
  • P-value (for each coefficient): whether that predictor is statistically significant.
  • R²: the proportion of variation in the outcome explained by the model, from 0 to 1.

The step-by-step method for interpreting output

  1. Check the coefficient's sign and size — positive means the outcome increases as the predictor increases; negative means it decreases.
  2. Check the p-value for that coefficient — below 0.05 typically means the relationship is statistically significant.
  3. Check R² to see how well the model fits overall — a significant coefficient doesn't mean the model explains much of the variation.
  4. Write the interpretation in plain language, connecting the number back to the real-world variables in the question.

Worked example

A regression predicting exam score from hours studied gives: coefficient = 4.2, p = 0.01, R² = 0.35

Interpretation: for each additional hour studied, exam score increases by an estimated 4.2 points on average. This relationship is statistically significant (p = 0.01 < 0.05). Hours studied explains about 35% of the variation in exam scores — a moderate but not complete fit, meaning other factors matter too.

Common mistakes

Treating correlation as causation. A significant regression coefficient shows an association, not proof that the predictor causes the change in the outcome — especially in observational (non-experimental) data.

Reporting the coefficient without checking R². A statistically significant predictor can still leave most of the variation unexplained. Both numbers matter for a complete interpretation, not just the p-value.
